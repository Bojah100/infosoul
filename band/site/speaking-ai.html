The AI age, for the layman.

What is happening, what it means, how to use it, and what is worth arguing about

One talk, for people who are not engineers. A plain-language orientation to the age we are already in, in five parts: where it came from, how it works, the questions it puts to us, who owns it, and how to use it well. Then one thing to do: run your own. From somebody who builds these systems for a living and is not selling you one from the stage.

“Everyone meets these machines at the same door, the engineer and the grandmother alike, and on the other side is something nobody fully understands and anyone can use. An enigma and an invitation.” — from the book behind the talk

“Whether or not the data centres are shut down, whether or not a model is banned, whether or not a company fails or a law passes, the thing itself is now known, to the people of the whole world. The method is in the papers, and the file is on drives in every country, copied more times than anyone can count. A file of numbers can talk back to you. That is a fact about the world now, like the fact that a lever lifts, and nobody gets to unknow it.”

What the room gets

  • A brief history of AI — where it actually came from: the chess computer on the coffee table and the game consoles whose chips became its engine, each new screen taking the last one’s place (video killed the radio star; the console emptied the arcade; now streaming, headsets and the feed that chooses), the physics and game engines that became the machines’ practice grounds, the robots from Shakey to Boston Dynamics, and why what is hard for us came easily to them; the machines we imagined first, from Robby, HAL and KITT to Tron, WarGames and I, Robot; Moore’s law, the winters and the breakthroughs, and why it arrived in public all at once when it had been with us all along: the day the numbers talked back. We grew up with it developing in the background, but right in front of our faces. And the campaigns that learned it first, in both parties: Dean’s online money, Obama’s email list, Trump’s Twitter and podcasts.
  • The questions — does it think, is it creative, is anything home, is it alive, and what would follow if it were. Turing, Searle’s room, the bat, the parrot, and the presenter’s teacher Harvey Sarles on language and the body; why each is still open, and where the presenter stands; and the fear everyone brings in, whether it will take over like Skynet.
  • The tech, broken down — in plain speech, no maths, only as far as the questions need it: how a machine learns, the cousins that make pictures, voices and video, and the open ones you can run yourself, and what the scores actually mean.
  • Using it — real cases, demonstrated live, including running a free model on your own laptop with no company in the middle.
  • What it gets wrong — sycophancy, and invention: a machine that produces a convincing record is not a nuisance when the record is somebody’s life.
  • Fakes, and the machine on the street — deepfaked voices and video calls, and the one habit that still works: check the channel, not the face. Driverless taxis on public roads, and who answers when the machine decides; and how cheap drones changed the balance between small powers and great ones, from the Red Sea to the Strait of Hormuz, as Nikola Tesla foresaw in 1900: “machine must fight machine.”
  • Biology, turned — from the genome project and CRISPR to AlphaFold and a model that judges every letter of our genes; the fruit fly whose whole nervous system is now mapped, and already put to work, simulated on a laptop, run on brain-like chips and walking a virtual body, and still does not answer the question of mind; computers built on living human neurons, an ethical grey zone; and the DNA of fifteen million people sold in a bankruptcy.
  • Three discoveries, and their implications — the protein atlas, the enzyme found in viral DNA, the claimed proof about flowing water; what they do to medicine, patents, security, universities and the locks every bank relies on, which almost nobody has put to the people they affect.
  • Who owns it, and what it means for work — the money, the data centres, what is displacing and what is newly possible without capital; why a machine that can do the job does not stop a strike, an organising drive or a public works programme, because the fight is political, and why Google’s own engineers could stop Project Maven until Palantir took it over; and why the warehouse may not be the only road to the frontier.
  • Where you meet it, and who can read it — the inbox summary, the search answer, the chat bubble on a website, the assistant in the group chat, the notetaker in the call, every social media feed, the camera glasses on a stranger’s face: four questions to tell what you are talking to, where it goes, who else can read it and whether it will teach the machine, and a privacy ladder from your own laptop to the public square.
  • Who is listening — one system across three administrations, from Total Information Awareness under Bush to what Snowden exposed under Bush and Obama; risk scores and AI-scanned calls in prisons, where people can least object; Palantir, joining the records into one picture; and open-source intelligence, the God’s Eye that anyone can now build from public data, because what the machines add is not new information but ease; and what stopped governments watching everyone was the labour, and these machines remove it: from Snowden’s disclosures to face recognition that arrested the wrong man, licence-plate searches for immigration officers and a new operating system for deportation. Under both parties; and why running your own, with its online features off, keeps what you type on your computer.
  • Small machines, moving power — from the fax machine and the VCR to the Arab Spring on Facebook and the London riots on BlackBerry Messenger, Ferguson’s phones, the video of George Floyd’s killing, organisers on Signal and WhatsApp, crowdfunding that let a movement pay its own way, and the 2025 raid warnings that ended when Apple pulled ICEBlock: personal media has moved power for thirty years, and the model on your own laptop is the next one.
  • The prosthetic and the microphone — the machine as a mental prosthetic, not a merge; why the Alexa generation feared the microphone and their grandchildren never will; and how to own the ear: open speech models that run on your own laptop, even as the chip makers build machines for the desk.
  • The dance — how the book behind this talk was made with a machine: the real prompts, the corrections in both directions, and what the machine got wrong. Not a god, not a con: a partner that needs checking, and most of all when it agrees with you.
  • What the owners believe — technocracy, the merge of people and machines, a manifesto that names its enemies: the beliefs of some of the people who own the machines, in their own words, and why the room, not the owners, should decide.

Not a sales pitch, not a doom lecture, not a demo. The room leaves able to use one, and able to tell when it is flattering them.
